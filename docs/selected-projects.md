## Three examples

### 1. Explain a technical problem clearly

**Problem:** Full Wi-Fi bars can leave someone wondering why a website still will not load.

**Built:** An original animated explanation showing the difference between the local Wi-Fi connection and internet access, followed by two simple comparison checks. Landscape and portrait layouts were composed separately, with captions carrying the explanation.

**Inspect:** [Watch the published Facebook reel](https://www.facebook.com/reel/1467846138733469) · [Download the landscape sample](../examples/wifi-vs-internet/landscape.mp4) · [Animation source](../examples/wifi-vs-internet/landscape-source.py) · [Export verification](../examples/wifi-vs-internet/verification.json).

**What it proves:** A completed technical-media sample with editable source and checked exports. The diagrams are illustrations; they do not diagnose a customer's network or establish advertising results.

**Why it matters:** You should be able to understand which connection was checked and what remains uncertain when asking for tech help.

### 2. Turn troubleshooting into a useful lesson

**Problem:** A beginner may try unrelated fixes before checking the evidence behind a domain-join error.

**Built:** A free, approximately ten-minute Active Directory/DNS exercise with a ticket, configuration comparison, questions, a sample answer and a self-check.

**Inspect:** [Read and try the free lesson](../lessons/ad-domain-discovery/README.md). The complete lesson source is that same Markdown file.

**What it proves:** A structured teaching sample that separates observations, a proposed change and validation. Its ticket and outputs are fictional. It is a paper exercise, not a tested Windows lab, certification or employment promise.

**Why it matters:** Good troubleshooting follows the evidence, limits the change and checks the result.

### 3. Keep field instructions in one place

**Problem:** Checklists and setup notes become difficult to use when they are scattered across documents.

**Built:** A mobile-oriented field-reference app prototype with guide drawers, clipboard actions and a browser-local planning tracker.

**Inspect:** [Read the HTML/CSS/JavaScript source](../index.html) · [Review the implementation and its limits](project-evidence.md#vexon-it-field-app-a-mobile-oriented-reference-prototype).

**What it proves:** An inspectable reference-tool prototype. Source was reviewed; browser interaction and accessibility testing were not established by that review. Use fictional records: authentication, secure customer storage, cloud synchronization and payment processing are not implemented.

**Why it matters:** A usable handoff helps you find the next step after a service visit.

## From the first message to handoff

1. **Describe the need.** Send the device or business task, your city, what should work and any deadline. Keep passwords and payment details out of the message.
2. **Agree on the job.** Confirm the deliverable, time limit, exclusions, price and appointment or delivery date. Larger work receives a separate quote.
3. **Work within that scope.** Review what can be verified. If the problem or request changes, agree on the next step before adding work.
4. **Receive a clear handoff.** Get the agreed output or a short record of checks, changes, unresolved items and practical next steps.
